Lab 5: Interactive Network Visualization

Back to home

A synthetic urban transit system drawn twice from the same two tables: once as a force-directed node-link diagram and once as an adjacency matrix.

1. Dataset Description

  • Stations: 50 stations, 10 in each of five districts (Central, North, South, East, West). Each station has a district, an approximate daily_passengers volume (1,373–9,850) and a station_type of Local, Transfer or Terminal.
  • Routes: 50 undirected direct connections. Each has a travel_time_min (2–16 minutes) and a route_type of Metro, Express or Shuttle.

2. Node-Link Visualization

Marks are labeled with the station number. Drag a station to move it, hover a station to isolate its direct neighbours, and hover a route to see its type and travel time.

3. Node-Link Design Description

Color is reserved for district: five hues are easy to tell apart, and a glance shows whether a route stays inside a district or crosses into another. Daily passengers maps to mark area, so a busy station is literally a bigger station, while station type uses shape (circle = Local, diamond = Transfer, square = Terminal), which stays legible at any size or color. Travel time controls both the rest length and the width of a link: a quick hop is drawn short and thick, a slow one long and thin, so a connection that is "strong" in transit terms also looks strong on screen. Route type is hue plus dash pattern, so the three services stay distinguishable even where a thin amber Express line runs beside an orange East station. Dragging lets the reader untangle a crowded region; hovering a station fades everything except its direct neighbours, hovering a route isolates its two endpoints, and tooltips give the exact passenger count, minutes and number of connections that the marks only approximate.

4. Adjacency Matrix

Rows and columns are stations; a filled cell is a direct route. Choose how rows and columns are ordered, and hover a cell or a label for details.

Cell color: Route type (same colors as above) Cell opacity: Travel time (quick = solid) Strip: District Glyph: Station type Bar: Daily passengers

5. Matrix Design Description

Cell hue repeats the route-type colors and cell opacity repeats the travel-time rule (quick = solid), so the two views share one legend. Each row carries a district strip, a station-type glyph and a passenger bar (columns get the strip and glyph), so all five attributes survive without crowding the cells. The buttons reorder rows and columns by ID, district, station type, passengers or connections, animating so cells can be tracked. District order is the default: separator lines cut the matrix into 5 × 5 blocks whose density is the number of routes between two districts, something the node-link view cannot show. ID order instead exposes the chain along the sub-diagonal, and connection order stacks the best-connected stations in the top-left corner.

6. Network Questions and Findings

Question Answerable? View Encoding / interaction used Observation
1. Which stations appear central? Well Both Node-link: position and hover highlighting (how many marks stay lit). Matrix: the "Connections" ordering. The network reads as one long loop with a handful of long, thin chords cutting across it. The stations 7, 31, 18, 34, 23,and 10 tie distant parts of the loop together and light up three neighbours on hover, so they are the most central. The connection-ordered matrix confirms that 14 stations have three routes and nobody has more.
2. Which districts are strongly connected? Well Matrix District ordering with separator lines The diagonal blocks are almost empty (only two Central–Central routes), so nearly every route crosses a district boundary. The densest blocks are East–South, followed by Central–North and East–West. Central–South, Central–East and North–East are completely empty.
3. Where are transfer / terminal stations in the topology? Partially Node-link Shape plus hover highlighting, and the "Station type" matrix ordering as a check. Terminals make up most of the three-route junctions where the chords meet the loop, but transfers are mostly ordinary two-route stations along the chain, and three of the five unconnected marks are also terminals. Station type does not line up cleanly with topological role, and the type-ordered matrix shows no block structure either.
4. Which stations have high passenger volume? Well Both Node-link: mark area. Matrix: "Daily passengers" ordering with the row bars. The largest marks (stations 45–50) all sit at the edge of the diagram, either dangling from a single chord or entirely unconnected, while the small marks form the dense core. In the passenger-ordered matrix the top rows have the longest bars and the fewest cells. In this dataset, the busiest stations are the least connected.
5. Where are the longest direct travel-time connections? Partially Both Node-link: thinnest and longest links, tooltip for minutes. Matrix: palest cells, and distance from the sub-diagonal in ID order. The three slowest routes are all thin amber Express chords that jump across the network: 7–31 (16 min), 18–34 (15 min) and 29–50 (14 min). In the ID-ordered matrix they are the faintest cells and sit far from the sub-diagonal, i.e. they connect stations that are far apart along the chain.
6. Are route types concentrated in particular parts? Well (the answer is no) Both Node-link: link hue and dash. Matrix: ID ordering and the district strips beside the colored cells. In ID order the sub-diagonal cycles Express, Shuttle, Metro in strict rotation along the chain, and every district's row band contains all three colors; in the node-link view the three hues are interleaved everywhere. The only pattern is that the long-range chords with the highest travel times are all Express.
