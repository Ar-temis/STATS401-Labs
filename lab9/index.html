Lab 9: Geospatial Visualization

Back to home

The 50 largest economies of 2025 drawn twice from the same data: a choropleth that keeps true geography and encodes GDP as color, and a contiguous cartogram that resizes every country so its area is proportional to GDP. Hovering or clicking a country in either map highlights it in both.

1. Dataset Description

  • GDP: data/lab9_gdp_2025_top50.csv, the 50 largest economies by 2025 GDP. Values range from $300 billion (Kazakhstan, rank 50) to $30.6 trillion (United States).
  • Boundaries: data/ne_110m_admin_0_countries.geojson, the unmodified Natural Earth 1:110m admin-0 countries file. After loading, Antarctica is removed and each feature gets a properties.iso3 code from ISO_A3.
  • Join: GDP rows are attached to features with a Map keyed by ISO-3. Countries outside the top 50 get value = null.

2. 2025 GDP Choropleth

Nominal GDP by country, 2025: color = GDP (log scale)

No data (not in top 50)

Equal Earth projection, so land areas are not inflated toward the poles. Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan.

3. 2025 GDP Cartogram

Nominal GDP by country, 2025: area = GDP

Each country's area is proportional to its 2025 GDP; the United States covers about a quarter of the map because it produces about a quarter of the top-50 total. Color repeats the choropleth scale only to help match countries across the two maps. Hatched countries are not in the dataset. Together they are given a fixed 5% of the map, split by land area, only so the map stays connected. Their size means nothing. Labels are ISO-3 codes.

4. Design and Comparison

Design choices

Projection and join. Both maps use d3.geoEqualEarth() fitted to the same 960 × 500 frame. Because Equal Earth preserves area, the choropleth does not inflate Canada, Russia or Greenland the way Mercator would, and the cartogram starts from true relative land areas. GDP is joined onto each feature by properties.iso3

Color scale. GDP is very skewed: the US alone is 28% of the 50-economy total and the median economy is only about $670 billion. With a linear scale only the US and China would be visibly blue and the other 48 economies would share nearly the same pale tint. I use d3.scaleSequentialLog with the Blues ramp (starting at 20% so the smallest economy is still clearly blue). Equal color steps then mean equal ratios, so mid-sized economies such as Poland, Saudi Arabia and Indonesia can be told apart. The trade-off is that a log scale visually compresses the gap at the top, which the cartogram then shows directly.

Cartogram. The cartogram uses the rubber-sheet algorithm of Dougenik, Chrisman & Niemeyer (1985), the algorithm behind the suggested topogram and cartogram-chart libraries, rewritten for D3 v7 in cartogram.js. Every border vertex is first projected with the same Equal Earth projection. In each iteration every country pushes vertices outward if it is smaller than its GDP share of the map, or pulls them in if it is larger. Shared borders are stored as shared vertices, so the map stays contiguous. The result is drawn with an d3.geoPath(). The 60 iterations are animated, so you can watch countries grow and shrink from their true shapes.

Linked views. Hover state and a click-to-pin selection live in one shared object, and every change restyles both maps. The dropdown helps find countries that are hard to spot in either map (Belgium or Israel in the choropleth, Canada or India in the cartogram), and each tooltip compares the country's share of GDP with its share of the cartogram's area.

Choropleth vs. cartogram

1. Which countries are most visually prominent in each map?

In the choropleth, the US and China (darkest), plus Russia, Canada, Brazil and Australia, whose sheer land area dominates even though together they produce less than China. In the cartogram, the US and China overwhelm everything, followed by Germany, Japan, the UK, India and France.

2. Which countries become much larger or smaller in the cartogram?

Small, rich economies grow most. For example: the Netherlands, Israel, Taiwan, South Korea, the UK, Germany and Japan. Land-rich economies shrink: Kazakhstan, Russia, Canada, Brazil, Australia and Argentina. Africa almost disappears.

3. What geographic information does the choropleth preserve?

Location, shape, true relative size and adjacency, so every country is recognizable.

4. What geographic information does the cartogram distort?

Shape, area and distance. India and Central Asia are squeezed into twisted slivers, and only rough position and neighbors survive.

5. What types of questions or tasks can the choropleth best support?

Where questions: where large economies cluster (North America, Western Europe, East Asia), which regions lack data, and how neighbors compare.

6. What types of questions or tasks can the cartogram best support?

How much questions: ranking economies, estimating shares of the total, and seeing how concentrated output is.

7. How does changing from color encoding to area encoding change your perception of global economic size?

On the log color scale Germany and Brazil look similar, but by area Germany is more than twice Brazil, and US–China dominance is unmistakable.
